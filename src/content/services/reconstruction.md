## What reconstruction services actually involve

When a pipe break, house fire, or roof collapse leaves a home or commercial building with torn-out drywall, charred framing, or a demoed section of flooring, the demolition is only half the job. Reconstruction services las vegas property owners need cover everything between the gutted shell and a space that passes final inspection: framing repairs, subfloor replacement, drywall and insulation, electrical and plumbing rough-in where walls were opened, paint, trim, flooring, and cabinetry. This isn't a remodel chosen for style. It's rebuild services tied directly to a documented loss, which means the scope has to match what the adjuster approved, and every change has to be traceable back to the original damage.

Timelines vary widely depending on how much structure was affected. A single bathroom after a slow leak might take one to two weeks once materials arrive. A kitchen fire that took out cabinetry, subfloor, and a load-bearing header can run six to ten weeks once permits, framing inspections, and finish work are factored in. Structural reconstruction involving engineered lumber or steel reinforcement adds engineering review time before a permit is even pulled.

## Our process

1. **Scope of work and documentation.** Before any material is ordered, we walk the damaged structure, photograph existing conditions, and write a line-item scope that separates mitigation work already completed from the rebuild itself. This scope is what gets submitted to the insurance carrier for approval.
2. **Permitting.** Structural work, electrical, and plumbing changes in Clark County and the City of Las Vegas typically require permits pulled before framing starts. We identify which trades need permits early so inspections don't stall the schedule later.
3. **Framing and rough-in.** Damaged studs, joists, and subfloor are replaced to match existing structure. Electrical, plumbing, and HVAC rough-in happens here if walls or ceilings were opened, and this is where a rough-in inspection is usually scheduled before anything gets closed up.
4. **Insulation, drywall, and finishes.** Once rough-in passes inspection, insulation goes back in, drywall is hung and finished, and paint, trim, flooring, and fixtures are installed to match the pre-loss condition or better, as approved in the scope.
5. **Final walkthrough and closeout.** A final inspection confirms code compliance, and we walk the space with the homeowner or property manager to compare finished work against the agreed scope before closing out the job.

## What separates a good reconstruction response from a bad one

The most common failure point isn't the carpentry, it's the handoff between mitigation and rebuild. If the crew that dried out the structure doesn't communicate clearly with the crew doing post-disaster rebuilding, moisture readings get lost, and framing gets closed up over studs or subfloor that never fully dried. That leads to warped flooring or musty odors months later, and it's one of the first things an adjuster or a second-opinion inspector will flag.

Another gap: scope creep that isn't documented. A homeowner asks for an upgraded countertop or a different tile layout mid-project, and if that change isn't logged against the original insurance scope, it creates a billing dispute at the end. A careful operator writes a change order and gets sign-off before proceeding, not after.

Adjusters also look closely at whether permits were actually pulled for structural and electrical work, not just promised. Unpermitted work can delay a home sale or trigger a claim denial down the line if it's discovered later. And they check whether fire damage reconstruction specifically addressed smoke-affected framing members, not just the visibly burned ones, since char can run deeper into structural wood than it appears.

## What does reconstruction cost?

Costs depend heavily on how much of the structure was demolished, whether load-bearing elements are involved, and local material and labor pricing. These are typical industry ranges, not a quote: every loss is different, and PuroClean of East Las Vegas provides a written scope of work before any reconstruction begins so you know what's included.

| Scenario | Typical range |
|---|---|
| Single bathroom, non-structural | $4,000 - $10,000 |
| Kitchen rebuild after fire or water loss | $15,000 - $45,000 |
| Structural framing repair (one room) | $8,000 - $20,000 |
| Whole-room reconstruction after total loss | $20,000 - $60,000 |
| Multi-room post-disaster rebuild | $50,000 - $150,000+ |

Most homeowners insurance policies cover reconstruction costs tied to a covered peril, such as fire, a burst pipe, or storm damage, up to the policy's dwelling coverage limit, though cosmetic upgrades beyond pre-loss condition are typically an out-of-pocket add.

## Seasonal & regional considerations

Las Vegas's dry climate is generally forgiving for construction timelines, with fewer weather delays than humid regions, but summer heat above 105 degrees can affect curing times for concrete, stucco, and some adhesives, which sometimes pushes exterior work to early morning hours. Many homes in the valley, particularly in older neighborhoods near downtown and in Henderson, were built in the 1960s through 1980s with materials and framing techniques that don't always match current code, so structural reconstruction in these homes often requires bringing adjacent, undamaged sections up to code as part of the permit approval, not just repairing what was lost.
