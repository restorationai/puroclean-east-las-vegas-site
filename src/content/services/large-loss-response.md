When a water main breaks in a 200-unit apartment complex, a kitchen fire spreads through a hotel's HVAC chase, or a monsoon downpour overwhelms a warehouse roof system, the damage isn't contained to one unit or one room. It spans floors, wings, or entire buildings, and it requires a response scaled to match: multiple crews working simultaneously, equipment logistics measured in truckloads rather than units, and documentation detailed enough to support a claim with six or seven figures attached. That's the work this page is about.

## What large loss and catastrophic response actually involves

Large loss restoration applies when a single event affects multiple structures, multiple units within a property, or square footage large enough that a standard residential crew and equipment package can't keep pace with the damage. Think multi-family housing, hotels and casinos, hospitals, schools, warehouses, and office campuses. The work itself uses the same core science as any water, fire, or storm loss, extraction, drying, soot and smoke mitigation, but at a different order of magnitude: dozens of air movers and commercial dehumidifiers running in zones, moisture mapping across thousands of square feet, and a project manager whose job is coordinating crews, equipment, and reporting rather than running a single room.

Timelines vary enormously based on scope. A one-building apartment loss with contained water intrusion might mitigate in a week or two. A multi-structure fire or a casino floor flood involving structural drying, contents handling, and reconstruction scoping can run for months before the property is fully restored. What stays constant is the need for a single point of contact managing the whole event instead of a patchwork of separate vendors.

## Our process

1. **Rapid scene assessment and command structure.** Before equipment moves in, the loss gets mapped: which units or zones are affected, what categories of water or contamination are present, and what life-safety or structural concerns need immediate attention.
2. **Multi-crew mobilization and equipment logistics.** Large loss work means scaling fast, bringing in enough extraction units, desiccant dehumidifiers, and air movers to treat every affected zone at once rather than sequentially, which matters because drying delays compound across square footage.
3. **Phased mitigation with zone-by-zone documentation.** Each unit, floor, or wing gets its own moisture logs, photos, and drying progress records. On a multi-million dollar claim, that granularity is what supports the eventual payout.
4. **Large loss adjuster and stakeholder coordination.** Property managers, general contractors, engineers, and the carrier's large loss adjuster all need consistent reporting. This step keeps everyone working from the same scope and timeline instead of conflicting assessments.
5. **Scope handoff to reconstruction.** Once drying and mitigation are complete, the documented scope transitions into rebuild planning, whether that's PuroClean of East Las Vegas's own reconstruction team or a general contractor brought in by the property owner.

## What separates a good large loss response from a bad one

The most common failure point is under-scaling. A contractor sized for single-family water losses, applying residential equipment counts to a 150,000-square-foot warehouse, falls behind the drying curve within days, and secondary damage (microbial growth, warped flooring, delaminating drywall) sets in across areas that should have dried cleanly. A good large loss response treats equipment logistics as a planning problem from hour one, not something figured out as the job goes.

The second failure point is documentation that doesn't hold up to a large loss adjuster's scrutiny. On claims of this size, carriers expect moisture logs tied to specific units or zones, photo documentation at each drying stage, and a scope that can be reconciled against the structure's actual square footage. Vague or inconsistent documentation is where large claims get delayed or disputed. What adjusters want to see is a paper trail that matches the physical work, zone by zone, with nothing missing.

## Seasonal and regional considerations

Las Vegas's monsoon season brings sudden, heavy rainfall onto flat commercial and multi-family roofs that are common throughout the valley's mid-century and newer commercial stock. Flat roof systems here are built for a dry climate and can fail quickly under flash flooding, turning a roof leak into a multi-floor catastrophic loss in a matter of hours. Extreme summer heat also accelerates secondary damage, warping flooring and curing mold faster once water intrusion occurs, which makes the speed of mobilization on a large loss even more important in this climate than in milder regions.
